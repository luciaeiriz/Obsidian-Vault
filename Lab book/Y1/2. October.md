
# Signal Doppler Measurement 

The aim of this experiment is to simulate the received Doppler signal for an
inter-satellite encounter and investigate how well the Doppler shift can be recovered
using an FFT. Notes on the maths here: [[Signal processing]]

The main idea behind this experiments is to see how errors are carried along, but also because we have previously assumed the measurement would be available. 

We will firstly consider two extreme cases: 

1. Two satellites in LEO with a geometry chosen to produce a large line-of-sight closing velocity.
2. Geometry for which the line-of-sight range-rate approaches zero.

Script sand codes for this section are inside the `"RODINN/Signals"` folder. 
### Assumptions
The experiment retains the idealised assumptions used in the previous Doppler analysis (September file):

- two-body dynamics;
- continuous line of sight;
- perfect satellite states and timestamps;
- no measurement noise;
- no clock or oscillator errors;
- no $J_2$, drag or other perturbations;
- no receiver or instrument limitations.

These assumptions allow the effect of the relative orbital motion and carrier frequency to be studied independently.

### Reference values

Section 6 of the PICTS AltNav report considers a maximum relative velocity of approximately $v_{\mathrm{rel,max}} = 15.21~\mathrm{km/s}.$

This value is used as a reference for constructing the high-relative-velocity LEO case.

The two carrier frequencies considered in the report are:

| Parameter | VHF | S-band |
|---|---:|---:|
| Carrier frequency | $150$ MHz | $3$ GHz |
| Reference maximum Doppler | $7.6$ kHz | $152.2$ kHz |
| Sampling frequency | $16.5$ kHz | $334.9$ kHz |

The simulated orbital geometry will determine the actual line-of-sight range-rate and therefore the actual Doppler shift. The values above are used as reference values rather than imposed results.

### VHF and S-band Doppler

The same orbital encounter is used for both carrier frequencies.

The resulting [[VHF vs S-band]] Doppler histories are calculated and compared with the
reference values from the PICTS report.

Since the S-band carrier frequency is 20 times the VHF carrier frequency, the
simulation should satisfy

$$
D_{\mathrm{S}}(t) = 20D_{\mathrm{VHF}}(t)
$$

for the same relative motion.

### Method

#### 1. Setup 
We deliberately know both satellite orbits, calculate the "true" Doppler, turn that Doppler into a simulated radio signal, and see whether an FFT can recover it. 

We make it so that both satellites travel roughly at $7.6\ {\rm km/s},$ having them move almost oppositely gives a relative speed of approximately $7.6+7.6\approx15.2\ {\rm km/s}.$

The two orbits have slightly different radii, so they **do not collide**. Their minimum separation is exactly $10\ {\rm km}.$

#### 2. Relative motion
Once we know the state of satellite A and B:

$\mathbf r_A,\quad\mathbf v_A, \quad \mathbf r_B,\quad\mathbf v_B,$

the code calculates

$\Delta\mathbf r = \mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A$   and   $\Delta\mathbf v = \mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_A.$

The range (how far apart the satellites are) is:

$\rho=\|\Delta\mathbf r\|.$

range-rate:

$\boxed{ \dot\rho = \hat{\boldsymbol\rho}^{T}\Delta\mathbf v }$

where

$\hat{\boldsymbol\rho} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf r}{\rho}.$

#### 3. Turn range-rate into Doppler
Now we use the equation we've already discussed (look at [[Signal processing]]):

$\boxed{ D(t) = -\frac{f_c}{c}\dot\rho(t) }$

with our convention that approaching gives positive Doppler.

We want to compare the two carrier frequencies from the PICTS study:

$f_{c,\rm VHF}=150\ {\rm MHz}$   and   $f_{c,S}=3\ {\rm GHz}.$

Since

$\frac{3\ {\rm GHz}}{150\ {\rm MHz}}=20,$

the S-band Doppler must be exactly 20 times the VHF Doppler for the same motion:

$D_S=20D_{\rm VHF}.$

#### 4. Create the signal 
We simulate the **baseband Doppler signal**.

The instantaneous frequency of that signal is $D(t).$

Therefore its phase must satisfy

$$\frac{d\phi}{dt}=2\pi D(t).$$

So

$$\phi(t) = 2\pi\int D(t)\,dt+\phi_0.$$

Then the complex signal is essentially

$$\boxed{ z(t)=e^{j\phi(t)}. }$$

#### 5. Use FFT to measure the Doppler

We assume we don't know the orbital truth. We take a short section of received signal and calculate its FFT. 

For a simple constant frequency signal we get a strong peak at the signal frequency. So the code calculates:

$$\boxed{ f_{\rm peak}=f_{\arg\max |X(f)|^2} }$$
When Doppler is approximately constant over the window, $f_{\rm peak}$ can be used as an estimate $\hat D$ of the local Doppler. When Doppler varies substantially within the window, $f_{\rm peak}$ is simply the dominant frequency of that finite signal segment and need not equal the instantaneous Doppler at the window centre.

And the FFT frequency spacing is approximately:

$$\Delta f=\frac{1}{T},$$

where $T$ is the observation-window length. 

Therefore, increasing the observation duration gives finer nominal frequency resolution. However, if Doppler changes significantly during the observation window, the signal is no longer approximately constant-frequency and its spectral energy becomes distributed over a range of frequencies. Thus, increasing $T$ improves FFT bin spacing but can make a single FFT peak less representative of the instantaneous Doppler.

#### Conclusion

This experiment simulated the complete chain from known relative orbital motion to an ideal received Doppler signal and its recovery using an FFT:

$\text{relative orbit} \rightarrow \dot\rho(t) \rightarrow D(t) \rightarrow s_{\rm BB}(t) \rightarrow \text{FFT}.$

The high-closing-rate LEO encounter produced a relative speed of approximately $15.2~\mathrm{km/s}$ and Doppler shifts approaching the PICTS reference values of $7.6~\mathrm{kHz}$ at VHF and $152~\mathrm{kHz}$ at S-band. At closest approach, the total relative speed remains large while the LOS range-rate and Doppler pass through zero, demonstrating that Doppler depends on the LOS projection of relative velocity rather than total relative speed.

A simple FFT peak provides a useful local Doppler estimate when the Doppler remains approximately constant over the observation window. Increasing the window duration improves the nominal FFT frequency spacing,

$\Delta f\approx\frac{1}{T},$

but can also increase the Doppler variation contained within the window. Around closest approach, where Doppler changes rapidly, the signal becomes chirp-like and its spectrum spreads across many frequencies. In this situation the dominant FFT frequency can differ substantially from the instantaneous Doppler at the centre of the window.

Therefore, Doppler measurement using an FFT involves a trade-off between **frequency resolution and local signal stationarity**. A single FFT peak should not automatically be interpreted as an instantaneous Doppler measurement when the Doppler varies significantly during the observation interval.



### Experiment 2 — Low-relative-velocity encounter

Two satellites moving almost together with very slightly different radii. 
#### Geometry

In Experiment 1, the two satellites travelled in approximately opposite directions, producing a large total relative velocity of approximately $\|\Delta\mathbf v\|\approx15.2~\mathrm{km/s}.$

For Experiment 2, two physically valid **prograde circular LEO trajectories** are used. The satellites therefore travel in approximately the same direction with similar orbital velocities.

The purpose of this geometry is to produce a much smaller total relative velocity,

$\|\Delta\mathbf v\| = \|\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_A\|,$

#### Conclusion
The low-relative-velocity encounter produces Doppler shifts of only a few hertz at VHF and tens of hertz at S-band. In this regime, very short FFT windows can fail to resolve the Doppler because the spacing between FFT bins is large compared with the frequency shift being measured. Increasing the observation duration gives a finer frequency grid and substantially improves the dominant-bin Doppler estimate, provided that Doppler remains sufficiently stationary over the longer window.

This complements Experiment 1. In the high-relative-velocity encounter, long windows became problematic where Doppler varied rapidly within the observation interval. In the present low-relative-velocity encounter, the dominant limitation is instead the coarse FFT frequency grid associated with short windows. Together, the experiments demonstrate the trade-off between

$\boxed{ \Delta f_{\mathrm{FFT}}\approx\frac1T }$

and

$\boxed{ \Delta D_{\mathrm{window}} = D_{\max}-D_{\min}. }$

A suitable observation duration must therefore provide sufficient frequency resolution while keeping the Doppler sufficiently stationary within the window. ### Comparison of Experiments 1 and 2

Experiments 1 and 2 investigated Doppler extraction using the same signal-generation and FFT-processing method but under two very different relative-motion regimes. Experiment 1 considered a high-relative-velocity encounter, whereas Experiment 2 considered two nearby prograde satellites with much smaller relative velocity

The two experiments expose **opposite limitations of finite-window FFT Doppler extraction**

#### Experiment 1 - rapidly varying Doppler
In the high-relative-velocity encounter, Doppler can change significantly during an FFT observation window, particularly around closest approach. Increasing the window duration $T$ gives finer nominal frequency spacing, but also means that a larger portion of the changing Doppler history is contained within the window.

The signal within the window therefore becomes less like a constant-frequency tone. Its FFT represents a **range of frequencies experienced during the window**, rather than simply the instantaneous Doppler at its centre.

Consequently, a longer observation window does not necessarily give a better estimate of instantaneous Doppler in this regime.

#### Experiment 2 - slow varying Doppler
Experiment 2 demonstrates the opposite problem. Here the Doppler changes relatively slowly, but its magnitude is small. Very short windows therefore have FFT bins that are too widely spaced to resolve the Doppler accurately.

For example, a $0.01~\mathrm{s}$ window gives $\Delta f_{\mathrm{FFT}}=100~\mathrm{Hz},$ which is much larger than the VHF Doppler of only a few hertz. The dominant FFT bin consequently remains at $0~\mathrm{Hz}$ even though the true Doppler is non-zero

Increasing $T$ reduces the bin spacing and allows the dominant FFT bin to follow the Doppler more closely. The staircase behaviour and corresponding sawtooth errors observed in the results are consistent with the continuous Doppler moving between discrete FFT bins

#### Combined interpretation
Together, the experiments demonstrate a trade-off between two quantities:

$\boxed{ \Delta f_{\mathrm{FFT}}\approx\frac{1}{T} }$

and

$\boxed{ \Delta D_{\mathrm{window}} = D_{\max}-D_{\min}}$


- For a **short observation window**,
	
	$T\downarrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta f_{\mathrm{FFT}}\uparrow,$
	
	so the frequency grid becomes coarse. This is particularly problematic when the Doppler itself is small, as demonstrated by Experiment 2.

- For a **long observation window**,

	$T\uparrow \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta f_{\mathrm{FFT}}\downarrow,$
	
	which improves the frequency grid. However, if Doppler varies significantly with time, the window can contain a larger Doppler excursion,
	
	$\Delta D_{\mathrm{window}}\uparrow,$
	
	and the assumption that the window represents approximately one frequency becomes less appropriate, as demonstrated by Experiment 1.

Therefore

**A shorter window $\rightarrow$ better local stationarity **

vs

**better frequency resolution $\rightarrow$ longer window**

The experiments therefore do **not** identify one universally optimal FFT duration. Instead, an appropriate observation window depends on the local Doppler behaviour: it must be sufficiently long to resolve the Doppler frequency, while sufficiently short that the Doppler does not vary excessively within the window.

#### Conclusion

Inter-satellite Doppler can be extracted from the simulated received signal using an FFT when the Doppler is sufficiently resolvable and approximately stationary over the observation window. The required window duration depends on the relative-motion geometry. Short windows can fail when Doppler is small because of coarse FFT-bin spacing, while long windows can fail to represent instantaneous Doppler when the frequency changes significantly during the window.
